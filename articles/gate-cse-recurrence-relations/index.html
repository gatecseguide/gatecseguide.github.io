Recurrence Relations for GATE CSE: Master Theorem and More

By MD ANISH AHAMADUpdated 1 Oct 20266 min read

Recurrence relations are asked in almost every GATE CSE paper, and the papers deliberately choose the forms the Master theorem does not cover. This guide sets out the three Master cases and where they stop applying, then the substitution and recursion-tree methods that handle the rest, with one short example each.

Key takeaways

  • Recurrences score 85.5 out of 100 in the book's evidence model, fourth among algorithm concepts and inside the Very High band.
  • The Master theorem needs a polynomial gap between the driving function and the critical exponent. Two of GATE's favourite recurrences sit in the gap.
  • Subtract-and-conquer forms, unequal splits and square-root recurrences are outside the Master theorem entirely.
  • The recent format is a numeric answer asking for the exponent, which carries no negative marking.
  • Counting recurrences from discrete mathematics use characteristic equations and are a separate skill.

Where recurrences sit in the paper

The official Algorithms syllabus line names asymptotic worst case time and space complexity along with divide-and-conquer as a design technique, which is where recurrences enter. Separately, the Engineering Mathematics line names recurrence relations under combinatorics.

Analyst compilations, which are not official and differ by 2 to 3 marks between sources, put Algorithms at roughly 8.3 marks per paper averaged over 2009 to 2026, with a reported range of about 6 to 11. Within that, expect one recurrence or loop-complexity item per shift. The reconstructed record lists Master-theorem forms in 2002, 2004 to 2006, 2008, 2009, 2012, 2014 to 2017, 2019 to 2021, and 2023 to 2026, and non-standard substitution forms across a similar spread.

The Master theorem, stated for the exam

For a recurrence where aaa subproblems of size n/bn/bn/b are solved plus a driving cost f(n)f(n)f(n), let the critical exponent be log⁡ba\log_b alogb​a:

T(n)=a T ⁣(nb)+f(n)T(n) = a\,T\!\left(\frac{n}{b}\right) + f(n)T(n)=aT(bn​)+f(n)
  • Case 1. The driving cost is smaller than the critical power by a polynomial factor, f(n)=O(nlog⁡ba−ε)f(n) = O(n^{\log_b a - \varepsilon})f(n)=O(nlogb​a−ε) for some ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0. The answer is the critical power, T(n)=Θ(nlog⁡ba)T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_b a})T(n)=Θ(nlogb​a).
  • Case 2. The driving cost matches the critical power up to a log factor raised to some power k≥0k \ge 0k≥0, f(n)=Θ(nlog⁡balog⁡kn)f(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_b a} \log^k n)f(n)=Θ(nlogb​alogkn). Where the Master theorem stops

The word "polynomial" in Cases 1 and 3 is the whole exam. A gap of a log factor is not polynomial, so those recurrences fall into the extended Case 2 or out of the theorem altogether.

Substitution and change of variable

When the subproblem size does not shrink by a constant factor, expand the recurrence a few levels and find the pattern.

For a recurrence that reduces the size by one and adds nnn each time, expanding gives the sum of the first nnn integers, which is quadratic. Reducing by one and adding log⁡n\log nlogn gives the log of nnn factorial, which is nlog⁡nn \log nnlogn. Reducing by one with two recursive calls and a constant doubles the work each level and gives 2n2^n2n.

T(n)=T(n−1)+n  ⇒  T(n)=∑i=1ni=n(n+1)2=Θ(n2)T(n)=T(n−1)+log⁡n  ⇒  T(n)=∑i=1nlog⁡i=log⁡(n!)=Θ(nlog⁡n)T(n)=2T(n−1)+c  ⇒  T(n)=Θ(2n)\begin{aligned} T(n) = T(n-1) + n &\;\Rightarrow\; T(n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} i = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} = \Theta(n^2) \\ T(n) = T(n-1) + \log n &\;\Rightarrow\; T(n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log i = \log(n!) = \Theta(n \log n) \\ T(n) = 2T(n-1) + c &\;\Rightarrow\; T(n) = \Theta(2^n) \end{aligned}T(n)=T(n−1)+nT(n)=T(n−1)+lognT(n)=2T(n−1)+c​⇒T(n)=i=1∑n​i=2n(n+1)​=Θ(n2)⇒T(n)=i=1∑n​logi=log(n!)=Θ(nlogn)⇒T(n)=Θ(2n)​

For square-root steps, change the variable. Write the input as n=2mn = 2^mn=2m. A step down to the square root halves mmm, so the recurrence becomes a halving one with constant work, whose solution is log⁡m\log mlogm. Substituting back, m=log⁡nm = \log nm=logn, so the answer is log⁡log⁡n\log \log nloglogn.

Recursion trees for unequal splits

When the two subproblems have different sizes, draw the tree and read off the cost per level. For a recurrence that splits into one third and two thirds with a linear driving cost, T(n)=T(n/3)+T(2n/3)+nT(n) = T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + nT(n)=T(n/3)+T(2n/3)+n, every level costs about nnn until branches start bottoming out. The shortest root-to-leaf path has length log⁡3n\log_3 nlog3​n, and the longest has length log⁡3/2n\log_{3/2} nlog3/2​n. Both are proportional to log⁡n\log nlogn, so the total is Θ(nlog⁡n)\Theta(n \log n)Θ(nlogn).

The same reading handles a split into one half and one quarter with a linear cost, T(n)=T(n/2)+T(n/4)+nT(n) = T(n/2) + T(n/4) + nT(n)=T(n/2)+T(n/4)+n. The per-level cost now shrinks geometrically, by a factor of 34\displaystyle \frac{3}{4}43​ each level, so the sum is dominated by the root and the answer is linear in nnn:

n(1+34+(34)2+⋯ )≤4n=Θ(n)n \left(1 + \frac{3}{4} + \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2 + \cdots\right) \le 4n = \Theta(n)n(1+43​+(43​)2+⋯)≤4n=Θ(n)

The rule to remember from these two: when the per-level cost is constant, multiply it by the number of levels; when it shrinks geometrically, the root dominates; when it grows geometrically, the leaves dominate.

Counting recurrences from discrete mathematics

The other family is asked under combinatorics, where you are given a linear recurrence with constant coefficients and asked for a closed form or a specific term. The method is the characteristic equation.

Suppose a sequence satisfies the rule that each term is five times the previous minus six times the one before, with the first two terms 1 and 4. The characteristic equation has roots 2 and 3, so the closed form combines 2n2^n2n and 3n3^n3n. Fitting the two starting values gives 2⋅3n−2n2 \cdot 3^n - 2^n2⋅3n−2n, and the third term checks.

an=5an−1−6an−2,a0=1, a1=4r2−5r+6=0  ⇒  r=2, 3an=α 2n+β 3n  ⇒  α+β=1, 2α+3β=4  ⇒  α=−1, β=2an=2⋅3n−2na2=2⋅9−4=14=5⋅4−6⋅1\begin{aligned} a_n &= 5a_{n-1} - 6a_{n-2}, \qquad a_0 = 1,\ a_1 = 4 \\ r^2 - 5r + 6 &= 0 \;\Rightarrow\; r = 2,\ 3 \\ a_n &= \alpha\,2^n + \beta\,3^n \;\Rightarrow\; \alpha + \beta = 1,\ 2\alpha + 3\beta = 4 \;\Rightarrow\; \alpha = -1,\ \beta = 2 \\ a_n &= 2 \cdot 3^n - 2^n \\ a_2 &= 2 \cdot 9 - 4 = 14 = 5 \cdot 4 - 6 \cdot 1 \end{aligned}an​r2−5r+6an​an​a2​​=5an−1​−6an−2​,a0​=1, a1​=4=0⇒r=2, 3=α2n+β3n⇒α+β=1, 2α+3β=4⇒α=−1, β=2=2⋅3n−2n=2⋅9−4=14=5⋅4−6⋅1​

Repeated roots change the shape: the closed form then multiplies one of the terms by nnn. The usual slips are a sign error in the characteristic equation and confusing which starting index the question uses. Counting problems, such as the number of binary strings with no two consecutive ones, reduce to this family after you set up the recurrence yourself, which is the harder half of the work.

How to practise recurrences

Keep the table above on one page and rewrite it from memory every week until the boundary rows are automatic. Then practise in the paper's own format, which is increasingly a numeric answer for an exponent or for a specific term rather than a choice among four asymptotic forms.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Master theorem enough for GATE recurrences?

No. It covers only recurrences of the divide-and-conquer form where the subproblem size is the input divided by a constant, and the comparison must differ by a polynomial factor. Subtract-and-conquer forms, unequal splits and square-root recurrences all fall outside it. GATE has repeatedly chosen exactly those forms, so substitution and recursion trees are needed too.

What is the answer for a recurrence with two halves and an nlog⁡nn \log nnlogn term?

It is Θ(nlog⁡2n)\Theta(n \log^2 n)Θ(nlog2n), not Θ(nlog⁡n)\Theta(n \log n)Θ(nlogn). This is the extended second case of the Master theorem, where the driving function matches the critical exponent up to a log factor raised to some power, and the solution gains one more log. Applying the basic form and answering nlog⁡nn \log nnlogn is one of the most repeated algorithm traps.

How often are recurrences asked in GATE CSE?

The reconstructed record shows a recurrence or loop-complexity item in essentially every paper. In the book's model, recurrences score 85.5 out of 100 and sit in the Very High band, ranked fourth among algorithm concepts. Typical weight is one item of 1 or 2 marks per shift, often as a numeric answer for the exponent.

Are recurrence relations part of mathematics or algorithms in GATE?

Both, under different names. The Engineering Mathematics syllabus names recurrence relations under combinatorics, where they are solved with characteristic equations for counting problems. The Algorithms syllabus reaches them through asymptotic analysis of divide-and-conquer methods. The techniques differ, so prepare both rather than assuming one covers the other.

How do I solve a recurrence where the size becomes a square root?

Change the variable. Substituting n=2mn = 2^mn=2m turns a square-root step into a halving step, so the recurrence becomes a familiar halving one and its solution is a log of a log, Θ(log⁡log⁡n)\Theta(\log \log n)Θ(loglogn). Then substitute back. The same trick handles recurrences that take a logarithm of the size at each step.
